Estimation is one of the hardest problems in software. We're asked to predict the future for work we've never done before, in systems we don't fully understand.

## Why Estimation is Hard

Software estimation fails for predictable reasons:

- **Uncertainty** — We're building something new; if we'd built it before, we'd reuse it
- **Unknown unknowns** — We don't know what we don't know until we start
- **Complexity** — Small changes can have large ripple effects
- **Optimism** — We consistently underestimate how long things take

The problem isn't that developers are bad at estimating. It's that accurate estimation of novel work is fundamentally difficult.

## Iterative Planning

Since we can't predict the future accurately, we plan in short cycles:

1. **Plan a small chunk** — One or two weeks of work
2. **Do the work** — Learn what's actually involved
3. **Review and adjust** — Update plans based on what you learned
4. **Repeat**

This is the heart of Agile planning. Instead of a detailed 6-month plan that will be wrong, you have a rough direction and detailed short-term plans that adapt.

## Relative Sizing

Many teams use story points instead of hours:

- Compare tasks to each other, not to absolute time
- "This task is about twice as complex as that one"
- Track velocity (points completed per iteration) to forecast capacity

This works because we're better at relative comparison than absolute estimation.

## Commitment vs Forecast

There's a difference between:

- **Forecast** — "Based on our velocity, we'll probably finish around March"
- **Commitment** — "We guarantee delivery by March 1st"

Forecasts are honest; commitments under uncertainty are often wishful thinking.

## Cargo Cult Planning

Watch out for teams that do Agile rituals without understanding them:

- **Sprints without iteration** — Fixed 2-week cycles, but no adjustment based on learning
- **Planning poker without discussion** — The point is the conversation, not the numbers
- **Velocity as a target** — Velocity measures capacity, not performance. Gaming it helps no one
- **Estimates as commitments** — Treating rough guesses as promises

The ceremonies exist to support the values. If you're doing the ceremonies without the values, you're missing the point.

## When Plans Change

Plans will change. Requirements shift, priorities change, you learn things that invalidate assumptions.

This is normal, not failure. The Agile Manifesto says: "Responding to change over following a plan."

The question isn't whether plans will change, but how gracefully you adapt when they do.
