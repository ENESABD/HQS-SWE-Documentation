**Matching Technology to Problem**

Every technology decision is a bet. You're betting that a tool will solve your problem better than the alternatives—and that the costs (learning curve, constraints, dependencies) are worth it.

## Questions to Ask

**What problem does it solve?** If you can't articulate this clearly, you're probably adopting technology for the wrong reasons.

**Do I have that problem?** Kubernetes solves orchestration at scale. If you have three servers, you probably don't need it.

**What's the learning curve?** A powerful tool you can't use effectively isn't powerful at all.

**What's the community like?** Active communities mean more resources, faster bug fixes, and continued development. Abandoned projects become liabilities.

**What are the escape hatches?** Can you drop down to lower levels when needed? Can you migrate away if the tool doesn't work out?

## Red Flags

- Choosing technology because it's trendy
- Adopting tools to pad your resume
- Over-engineering for problems you don't have
- Ignoring the boring, proven option

## The Boring Technology Rule

Dan McKinley's "Choose Boring Technology" argues that every team has a limited budget for complexity. Spend it on your actual business problems, not on exciting new databases.

PostgreSQL, Linux, and Python aren't exciting. They're also incredibly reliable, well-documented, and understood by most developers. Sometimes boring is exactly right.

## When to Adopt New Technology

New tools earn their place when:

- They solve a problem you genuinely have
- The benefits clearly outweigh the learning and integration costs
- You have time to learn them properly
- You've evaluated alternatives

Otherwise, stick with what you know.
